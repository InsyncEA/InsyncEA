How Much Does an Executive Virtual Assistant Cost in 2026?

August 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A client once told me she almost didn't hire an executive assistant because she'd seen a listing for $8 an hour and assumed anyone who could actually run her calendar would cost triple that. She was right about the second part, but not for the reason she thought. The $8 listing wasn't cheap because the market was generous. It was cheap because it bought her someone with no experience managing a founder's schedule, and she found that out three weeks in when a client call got double-booked with her kid's parent-teacher conference.

Pricing for executive virtual assistants is confusing because the range is genuinely huge, and most of the guides out there just throw a number at you without explaining why the spread exists. Here's what actually drives the cost, what you should expect to pay at each level, and where people quietly overpay for things that don't matter.

The Real Price Ranges

Strip out the outliers and the market roughly sorts into three tiers:

  • Entry-level VAs handling basic admin: often somewhere in the $8 to $15 an hour range, usually for scheduling, data entry, and simple research with heavy oversight needed.
  • Experienced executive assistants: typically $20 to $45 an hour, depending on region and specialization, for someone who can run your calendar and inbox with minimal supervision and make judgment calls within clear boundaries.
  • Senior EAs or those with niche skills (bookkeeping, project management, executive-level travel, CRM systems): often $45 to $75+ an hour, reflecting both the skill stack and the trust required.

Agencies that vet, train, and provide backup coverage generally sit at the higher end of a tier compared to hiring a freelancer directly, and that premium is often worth it, which I'll get into below. For a sense of where in-house salaries currently land, Robert Half publishes updated executive assistant salary ranges each year that are worth checking against whatever quote you're comparing.

Hourly vs. Retainer: Which Actually Saves You Money

Founders often assume hourly billing is safer because you only pay for what you use. In practice, retainer packages tend to work out cheaper for anyone using an EA consistently, because the per-hour rate on a retainer is usually discounted against the pay-as-you-go rate, and you're not paying a premium for the unpredictability of your workload.

The math only favors hourly billing if your need is genuinely sporadic, like a founder who only needs travel booked twice a quarter. If you're using an EA every week, a retainer of 10 to 20 hours almost always beats stacking up hourly invoices.

What Actually Drives the Price Up (and What Doesn't)

Here's where it gets useful. Some things that sound like they'd cost more, don't much. Some things that sound minor, quietly do.

  • Timezone overlap costs more than you'd think. An EA who can work your exact business hours in real time, rather than asynchronously, tends to command a higher rate, especially across US-UK time differences.
  • Specialized software knowledge barely moves the price. Most EAs can learn a new tool like Asana or HubSpot within a week or two, so paying extra specifically because someone "already knows" your CRM is rarely worth it.
  • Confidentiality and financial access raise the rate, and should. An EA with access to your bank logins, invoicing, or bookkeeping is taking on more liability, and that's typically reflected in cost.
  • Years of experience matter less than years working specifically with founders. An EA with eight years in corporate admin but zero experience with a fast-moving startup often needs more hand-holding than someone with three years supporting scrappy small businesses.

The Hidden Costs Nobody Mentions

The sticker rate isn't the whole picture. A few things catch people off guard:

  • Onboarding time. The first two to four weeks are rarely as efficient as month two, because you're both still building shared shorthand. Budget for a slower ramp-up rather than expecting instant full productivity.
  • Tool subscriptions. If your EA needs their own seat on a project management tool like Asana or a scheduling tool, that's a small recurring cost on top of their rate.
  • Backup coverage. Freelance EAs going on vacation can leave you without support for a week or two. Agency-based EAs typically have backup coverage built in, which is part of what you're paying the premium for.

A Realistic Example

Take a founder running a 12-person consulting firm who hires an EA for 15 hours a week at $35 an hour through an agency. That's roughly $2,100 a month. Compare that to hiring an in-house junior admin at even a modest salary once you factor in payroll tax, benefits, a laptop, and desk space, and the virtual arrangement usually comes out significantly cheaper, while covering more specialized work than a junior in-house hire typically could.

How to Know You're Not Overpaying (or Underpaying)

A simple gut check: if your EA is consistently doing work that requires real judgment (rescheduling on your behalf, drafting client responses in your voice, catching problems before you see them) and you're paying entry-level rates, you're likely to lose them to someone else within a year. If your EA is only doing basic data entry and scheduling and you're paying senior rates, you're paying for a skill level you're not actually using.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to hire an executive virtual assistant or a full-time in-house EA?
A virtual EA is almost always cheaper once you account for salary, benefits, payroll tax, equipment, and office space that come with an in-house hire, and most founders don't need someone full time anyway.

What's a reasonable number of hours to start with?
Ten to fifteen hours a week is a common starting point for founders testing the arrangement, with scope narrowed to calendar and inbox management before expanding further.

Why do some executive assistants cost so much more than others?
Rate differences usually come down to experience level, specialization such as bookkeeping or project management, timezone overlap, and whether the assistant works through an agency with vetting and backup coverage built in.

The Bottom Line

The cheapest option on paper is rarely the cheapest option in practice, and the client I mentioned earlier ended up paying for two EAs in her first two months (the $8 one, and the $30 one she hired to fix what the first one missed). Price the role based on the judgment it requires, not just the task list, and you'll land closer to what it's actually worth.
