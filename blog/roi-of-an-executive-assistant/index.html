How to Measure the ROI of an Executive Assistant

August 6, 2026 · 9 min read

Ask most founders whether their executive assistant is worth what they're paying, and you'll get a vague, confident "yes," followed by silence when you ask them to actually show the math. That's not a criticism, it's just how most delegation decisions get evaluated: on feeling, not on numbers. Which is fine until budget season, or a difficult quarter, or a moment where every expense gets scrutinized and "it just feels worth it" isn't a strong enough answer to defend the line item.

Why ROI Gets Skipped for This Specific Hire

Founders track ROI obsessively for ad spend, for sales hires, for nearly every other cost center in the business. ASAP's ongoing research on administrative professional value has pushed back on this gap directly, arguing that admin support deserves the same rigor other cost centers get. EA support tends to get a pass, partly because the benefit feels diffuse (less stress, fewer dropped balls) rather than a clean number like "this campaign generated X in revenue." That diffuseness is real, but it doesn't mean the value can't be measured, it just needs a slightly different framework.

The Core Formula

At its simplest, EA ROI comes down to comparing two numbers:

Hours reclaimed per week × your effective hourly value doing higher-leverage work, compared against the EA's weekly cost.

If a founder reclaims 10 hours a week and their time doing revenue-generating work is worth meaningfully more per hour than the EA's rate, the reclaimed value clears the cost easily, often by a wide margin. This is a simplified version of the calculation, but it's a useful starting point, and it's more rigorous than most founders ever actually run.

What Counts as "Reclaimed Time"

This is where the calculation gets murky if you're not careful. Reclaimed time isn't just "hours the EA spent working," it's hours the founder would otherwise have spent on the same tasks, now freed up for something else. A useful gut check: for each task handed off, ask whether you would have personally done it yourself, at what quality, and how long it would have taken you. That's the real baseline, not the EA's time spent, but your time saved.

Beyond the Basic Formula: What's Harder to Quantify But Still Real

  • Reduced error rate. A missed follow-up or a double-booked meeting has a real cost, sometimes a large one, even though it's hard to put a precise number on it. Forbes' coverage of executive productivity has noted similar patterns when looking at how small operational failures compound over a year.
  • Faster response times to clients or partners. An inbox triaged same-day rather than every few days can directly affect relationships and deals, even if the effect doesn't show up as a clean line item.
  • Decision fatigue reduction. Fewer low-value decisions during the day tends to improve the quality of the high-value ones, a real but genuinely hard-to-isolate effect.
  • Capacity for growth work. Time reclaimed often goes toward things with no immediate, measurable payoff, relationship building, strategic thinking, that still meaningfully compound over time.

These don't fit neatly into the core formula, but ignoring them entirely understates the real value. A reasonable approach: calculate the hard number from the core formula, then treat these as a qualitative addition that tips the decision further in favor when the hard number is already close to break-even.

A Worked Example

A founder whose consulting work bills at a solid hourly rate hires an EA for 15 hours a week at a fraction of that rate. If even half of those 15 hours translate into the founder doing an equivalent amount of billable work they otherwise wouldn't have had time for, the reclaimed value alone covers the EA's full cost several times over, before even counting the reduced errors, faster client response times, or the mental bandwidth freed up for actually growing the business.

When the ROI Genuinely Isn't There Yet

Sometimes the math doesn't clear, and it's worth being honest about when that's happening rather than assuming it always will eventually. Common reasons ROI stays weak past the normal ramp-up period:

  • The EA is stuck on low-leverage tasks rather than the highest-friction parts of the founder's week
  • Scope was never clearly defined, so time gets spent re-explaining rather than executing
  • The founder hasn't actually let go of the tasks that were handed off, still double-checking or redoing work, which erases most of the time savings

In most of these cases, the fix is a scope or trust conversation, not necessarily ending the arrangement, though it's worth reading the signs that point to a genuine mismatch before assuming it's just a rough patch.

How Long Before ROI Should Be Visible

Most founders see a real, noticeable shift by week six to eight, once onboarding is genuinely complete and the EA is operating with real autonomy rather than needing constant direction. Measuring ROI in week two, while everything is still being set up, will understate the eventual value considerably.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's a simple formula for calculating EA ROI?
Multiply the hours reclaimed per week by your effective hourly value doing higher-leverage work, then compare that figure against the EA's weekly cost. If reclaimed value exceeds cost, the arrangement is paying off in the most basic sense, though the fuller picture includes harder-to-quantify factors too.

How soon should I expect to see ROI from a new EA?
Most founders see a meaningful shift by week six to eight, once onboarding is complete and the assistant is operating with real autonomy rather than needing constant direction.

What if the ROI doesn't seem clear after a few months?
Unclear ROI after a reasonable ramp-up period is often a scope problem rather than a hiring mistake. It usually means the EA has been kept on low-leverage tasks rather than given ownership of the highest-friction parts of the founder's week.

The Bottom Line

"It feels worth it" is a fine gut check, but it's not a defensible answer when the budget gets questioned. Run the actual numbers, hours reclaimed against effective hourly value, compared to cost, and layer in the harder-to-quantify factors honestly. Most founders who actually do this math are surprised the number is stronger than the vague feeling suggested, not weaker.
