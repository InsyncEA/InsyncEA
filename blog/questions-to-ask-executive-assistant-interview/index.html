Questions to Ask When Hiring an Executive Assistant

August 6, 2026 · 10 min read

Most executive assistant interviews test the wrong thing. They confirm that a candidate is polished, articulate, and has a résumé full of the right software and past titles, none of which reliably predicts whether they'll actually be good at making judgment calls on your behalf when you're not in the room. The skills are learnable in weeks. The judgment either exists or it doesn't, and most standard interview questions never actually get at it.

Here's a set of questions built to surface judgment, discretion, and working style, along with what to actually listen for in the answers, not just whether they gave one.

Why Standard Interview Questions Fall Short

"Tell me about your experience with calendar management" invites a rehearsed answer. Almost every candidate has one ready, and it tells you almost nothing about how they'll actually behave the first time a scheduling conflict has no clean answer. SHRM's interviewing best practices make a similar point about structured, scenario-based questions outperforming generic ones. The goal of an EA interview isn't confirming competence with the tools, it's finding out how someone thinks when a situation doesn't have an obvious right answer.

Judgment Questions

"Tell me about a time you made a decision on someone's behalf without checking in first. What happened?"

This is the single most useful question in the entire interview. It surfaces real examples of judgment in action, and the follow-up matters just as much as the initial answer, ask what would've made them check in instead, and what they learned from how it turned out. Candidates who've genuinely operated with autonomy usually have a specific story ready. Candidates who haven't tend to answer in generalities.

"Describe a situation where you disagreed with your manager's instructions. What did you do?"

You're not looking for blind compliance or stubborn pushback, you're looking for someone who can voice a concern clearly and then execute the decision once it's made, even if it wasn't the direction they'd have chosen themselves.

"Walk me through how you'd handle two urgent, conflicting requests arriving at the same time."

There's no perfect answer here, what matters is whether they have a reasoning process at all, versus freezing or defaulting to whichever request arrived first.

Discretion and Trust Questions

"Have you ever had access to confidential information in a past role? How did you handle it?"

Listen for specificity. Vague reassurances about being "very trustworthy" are worth far less than a concrete example of a situation where discretion actually mattered and they handled it well.

"What would you do if you made a mistake that affected me directly, and I hadn't noticed yet?"

This question reveals more about self-protection instincts than almost any other. You want someone who says they'd flag it immediately, not someone who talks around whether they'd bring it up at all.

Working Style Questions

"What does your ideal working relationship with an executive look like?"

Pay attention to whether they describe wanting clear instructions for everything, or whether they describe wanting enough context to make calls independently. Neither answer is automatically wrong, but it needs to match what you're actually looking for.

"Tell me about a time your workload changed suddenly. How did you reprioritize?"

This gets at adaptability under pressure, which matters enormously in a role where priorities shift constantly and rarely on a predictable schedule.

The Scenario Exercise That Beats Every Interview Question

Talking about hypothetical judgment is different from demonstrating it. A short, practical exercise, five to ten minutes, built into the interview reveals far more than another round of questions:

  • Give them a sample inbox with five emails of varying urgency and ask them to sort and draft a quick response plan for each.
  • Present a mock daily schedule with a scheduling conflict and ask them to resolve it, explaining their reasoning out loud.
  • Ask them to draft a two-line message declining a meeting request in a tone that's polite but firm.

How someone thinks through these small, realistic tasks tells you more in ten minutes than an hour of talking about past experience, a point Indeed's guide to interviewing candidates also emphasizes when comparing behavioral questions against practical assessments.

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Every answer is a generality with no specific example attached
  • They describe every past employer or manager negatively, without any self-reflection on their own role in the friction
  • They can't describe a situation where they made an independent call, even a small one
  • They seem more focused on the tools they know than on how they think through ambiguous situations

What a Strong Answer Actually Sounds Like

Strong candidates tend to answer judgment questions with a specific story, a clear explanation of their reasoning at the time, and an honest reflection on what they'd do differently, if anything. They don't need the story to have a perfect outcome. What matters is that they can walk through their own thinking clearly, because that's the same thinking they'll be doing on your behalf when there's no time to ask you first.

Structuring the Interview Process

For most hires, two rounds cover what you need: one conversational interview focused on communication style and general fit, followed by a scenario-based round built around the judgment questions and practical exercise above. A third round is rarely necessary unless the role involves an unusually high level of trust, such as direct financial access or managing highly sensitive information, in which case a reference check focused specifically on discretion is worth the extra step. Once you've made the hire, setting up the working relationship well matters just as much as who you picked.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the single most revealing question to ask an executive assistant candidate?
Asking them to describe a time they made a judgment call without checking in first, and what happened, tends to reveal more than any skills-based question, because it shows how they actually think under ambiguity rather than what they claim they'd do.

Should I give candidates a practical test during the interview?
Yes. A short scenario exercise, like drafting a reply to a sample email or prioritizing a mock daily schedule, shows real working style in a way that talking about experience never fully captures.

How many interview rounds should hiring an executive assistant take?
Two rounds are usually enough for most hires: one conversational round to assess communication and fit, and one scenario-based round to test judgment. A third round only makes sense for very senior or high-trust EA roles.

The Bottom Line

The right questions don't confirm what's already on the résumé, they surface how someone actually thinks when a situation doesn't have a clean answer. Ask for specifics, watch for reasoning rather than just outcomes, and build in a real exercise rather than relying purely on conversation. That combination tells you far more about who you're actually hiring than any list of software skills ever will.
