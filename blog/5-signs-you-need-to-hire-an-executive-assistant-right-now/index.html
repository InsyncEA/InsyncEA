Executive Assistant for Solo Founders: What It Actually Looks Like

August 6, 2026 · 9 min read

Solo founders tend to assume an executive assistant is something you earn the right to once there's a real team, real revenue, real proof the business deserves the overhead. That instinct is understandable, and it's also backwards. A founder with no employees yet has nobody to absorb the administrative overflow, no ops person quietly handling scheduling, no office manager fielding routine questions. Solo founders often have the least bandwidth of anyone at any stage of a company, precisely because there's no one else. (If you're still weighing whether this applies to you, these five signs are a useful gut check.)

The Assumption Worth Questioning

The usual logic goes: hire an EA once you can "justify" it with team size or revenue. But justification isn't really about company size, it's about where your time is actually going. A solo founder spending eight hours a week on scheduling, inbox triage, and administrative busywork is losing that time regardless of whether there's a team around them. If anything, the case for an EA is stronger at this stage, because every hour lost to admin is an hour that direct-to-revenue work (sales calls, product decisions, client relationships) didn't get.

What This Actually Looks Like in Practice

For a solo founder, an EA relationship rarely starts as a full-time hire, and it doesn't need to. A common, workable starting point:

  • 5 to 10 hours a week, focused narrowly on calendar and inbox, the two highest-friction areas for most solo founders
  • Async-first communication, since a solo founder's day rarely has room for a lot of live check-ins
  • A short weekly sync, 15 to 20 minutes, to review priorities and catch anything that needs a judgment call
  • Scope that expands gradually, adding travel coordination or light research once the calendar and inbox relationship feels steady

This is a meaningfully smaller commitment than the "full admin team" image the term "executive assistant" sometimes conjures, and that's exactly the point. The role scales to the founder, not the other way around.

The Real Cost-Benefit for a Solo Founder

The relevant comparison isn't "can I afford ten hours a week of an EA's time." It's "what am I not doing while I spend those same ten hours on scheduling and email." For a founder whose time is worth meaningfully more per hour doing revenue-generating work than the EA's hourly rate, the math tends to favor delegating well before it feels comfortable to do so. A founder running a one-person consulting practice, for instance, might bill several hundred dollars an hour for client work while spending unpaid hours a week on calendar admin that could be handled at a fraction of that rate.

What Solo Founders Get Wrong When They Try This

The most common mistake isn't hiring too early, it's hiring without narrowing scope first. Entrepreneur's coverage of early-stage delegation flags this same pattern often: a founder trying to hand off everything at once (calendar, inbox, research, social media, light bookkeeping) in the first week overwhelms both sides and usually collapses back into doing everything themselves within a month. Starting narrow, one or two systems, mastering those, then expanding, holds up far better than trying to offload the entire administrative load in one go.

A Realistic Starting Scenario

Picture a solo founder running an e-commerce brand who's been manually replying to customer emails, booking her own supplier calls across three time zones, and losing entire afternoons to a calendar that never quite reflects what she actually needs. Ten hours a week of EA support, focused purely on inbox triage and calendar management, doesn't touch her product or her marketing at all. What it does is return roughly ten hours a week directly to the parts of the business only she can run, which for a business this size is often the difference between staying reactive and finally getting ahead.

When to Expand Beyond the Basics

Once the initial calendar and inbox relationship is running smoothly, usually somewhere around the six to eight week mark, it's worth revisiting scope. Common next additions for solo founders include light research (competitor tracking, vendor comparisons), meeting prep, and basic financial administration like organizing receipts and expense tracking. SCORE's small business mentorship resources are a useful place to think through which of these to prioritize first based on where your specific business is bottlenecked.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it too early to hire an EA if I'm a solo founder with no team yet?
Not necessarily. Solo founders often have the least administrative bandwidth of anyone in a growing company, since there's no one else to absorb the overflow, which makes an EA relationship valuable earlier than most people assume.

How many hours a week does a solo founder typically need from an EA?
Many solo founders start with 5 to 10 hours a week focused on calendar and inbox alone, which is often enough to meaningfully reduce the administrative load without a large budget commitment.

Can a solo founder afford an executive assistant?
Often, yes, especially with a part-time or hourly arrangement rather than a full-time hire. The relevant comparison isn't the EA's rate in isolation, it's that rate against the revenue-generating work the founder isn't doing while stuck on admin.

The Bottom Line

Waiting for a team to "justify" an EA gets the logic backwards. Solo founders are often the ones with the least slack to absorb admin work, which makes a narrow, well-scoped EA relationship one of the highest-leverage early decisions available, not a milestone to defer until later.
